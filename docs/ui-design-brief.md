# Fabula UI/UX design brief

## The product

Fabula is a realtime roleplay platform where every character is played by an AI model and the human is the director. A session is called a scene. A scene has a premise, a world, a tone, content boundaries, a presentation style, and a cast of structured roles. The story is told in beats, one narrative turn at a time, by one role at a time. The human never has to write the story alone. At any moment they can pick one of two to four AI-written continuations, drop an out-of-character director note, or step into any role and write a line themselves. Every choice can branch, and nothing is ever destroyed. The product is a director's console wrapped around a literary experience, not a chat box with character cards.

The emotional target is the feeling of sitting in a rehearsal room with a talented troupe that is already in character. The interface should feel like a stage: dark, warm, focused, a little theatrical, with the prose as the brightest thing on screen. It should also feel like a console: the director can see who is about to speak, what the current run is doing, how much context the scene remembers, how much it has cost, and where every branch went, without leaving the story. The product's promise is control without interruption and surprise without chaos. The visual language that has grown around it is a dark stage with warm amber, parchment-colored text, serif prose, a small sans-serif control layer, and an eight-slot identity palette that gives every role a stable color used for avatars, beat borders, gutter markers, and branch nodes.

## Experience principles and non-negotiable laws

The UI is built on laws that must survive any redesign. Picking a path at the active tip extends the current branch. Picking a path that was offered somewhere earlier forks a new branch from that point, and the current branch remains untouched. Editing, rewriting, or regenerating a beat always forks from its parent, never edits in place. Switching branches recomputes memory only, with no regeneration and no destruction. Undo covers the director's story actions. These laws are not implementation trivia; they are the trust model of the product, so a redesign must make forks, originals, ghosts, and reversibility visible and understandable rather than hiding them behind a generic "history" menu.

The scene's committed beats are the source of truth, and streaming is a performance layer over them. Text arrives token by token and survives a reload because deltas are persisted. The interface must therefore distinguish four visual states of a beat: a live stream with a caret, a finished canonical beat, a failed attempt that never entered the active path, and an aborted partial stored as an artifact. It must also distinguish a pending pick from a committed one, and it must never show the same text twice. The current UI handles this with a streaming row that is replaced in place by the committed beat, a failed row with a retry affordance, an optimistic picked beat that pulses until it lands, and a toast with undo after the commit.

The three direction modes are the heart of the interaction model: pick a path, steer from the wings, write in character. They must remain available at almost every moment, and they must remain legible as three different intentions rather than three tabs of a generic text box. Path options have to render inline under their parent beat, never in a modal, because the choice is part of reading the scene. Steering is out of character and applies to upcoming beats. Writing is in fiction and becomes canon immediately. The distinction between those two acts is one of the most important things a redesign can clarify.

The product is guest-first. A visitor can create a scene, cast a troupe, generate an opening, and play without creating an account. Anonymous Convex identities persist in the browser. The only external dependency of the entire stack is the AI provider API; the application is self-hostable with Docker and Convex, which means the UI must not assume managed services, cloud-only auth screens, or a single vendor's account system. The interface language is part of the product: the visible vocabulary is scene, role or character, beat, path option, branch, floor, director, and cast. Internal words such as thread, agent, run, candidate, and projection do not appear in user-facing copy.

Accessibility is an acceptance criterion, not a polish pass. The shipped product is tested against WCAG 2.2 AA, including contrast-tested color pairs in both themes, keyboard operability of the full audition and play loop, focus traps in sheets and dialogs, a skip link, landmarks, polite live regions for streaming, reduced-motion variants for every animation, and a keyboard drag-and-drop path for reordering roles. Responsive design is likewise a first-class requirement: the play surface is expected to work from a 320px phone to an ultrawide desktop, with the reading column staying at a comfortable measure while side surfaces become sheets.

## Current information architecture

The shipped application has eight route-level surfaces. The root route is the value screen and landing page. `/new` is the three doors page. `/new/quick` is the one-line quick start and, after generation, the compact audition. `/new/wizard` is the four-step guided setup and the full audition. `/new/templates` is the genre gallery. `/s/[sceneId]` is the live play console. `/s/[sceneId]/archive` is the read-only replay, branch explorer, ledger, share, and export surface. `/share/[token]` is the shared live view, with viewer or director permission encoded in the token. A not-found page and route-level error boundaries cover the failure cases.

Every route sits inside a global application shell. The shell has a slim, sticky top bar with the Fabula wordmark and sparkle mark on the left, a command palette trigger labeled Search with a keyboard hint, a theme menu with light, dark, and system options, a keyboard shortcuts button, and an offline or reconnecting banner that appears under the bar when the connection changes. The shell can technically host a left pane and a right pane, but in the shipped routes the play console implements its own cast rail and inspector inline, so the scene screen ends up with two stacked headers: the global marketing and utility bar, then a scene toolbar with the title, status, premise, and scene actions. The rest of the application is a single column inside the shell. The shell also owns the command palette, the shortcuts dialog, and the skip link.

Around those pages sit the overlays that carry much of the product's interaction: the command palette, the keyboard shortcuts dialog, the share dialog, the export dialog, the role editor sheet, the sample-line sheet, the edit-beat dialog, three confirmation dialogs for regenerate, ghost-path fork, and wrap-and-archive, the inspector sheet on small screens, and the cast sheet on small screens. These are not separate pages, but a designer should treat them as first-class surfaces because they carry the highest-stakes copy and the densest controls.

## The entry experience

The landing page is a value-first onboarding screen with no account wall. At the top is a guest or demo banner that explains where scenes live. The hero says "Every character is AI. You are the director." with a short paragraph about choosing direction, steering, or stepping into a role, and two calls to action: Start a scene and Browse templates. Below the hero is a preview of a troupe, three sample role cards with monogram avatars, story roles, and hooks, introduced as "A troupe like this walks in." Below that are three cards explaining the three directing modes, followed by a wide card explaining that branching is additive by law, and a quiet footer about self-hosting. The page is calm and editorial, with a serif hero and generous spacing, and it sets the promise before asking for anything.

The three doors page is the transition from promise to action. Its heading is "Choose how you want to walk in." It presents three large cards. Quick start promises one line and then play, with the promise of three screens or fewer to a playable opening. Guided setup promises four steps, tone dials, presentation style, and a human role editor. Templates promises six genre seeds with premise, tone, and a cast skeleton. Each card has an icon, a tagline, a short description, and small capability chips. The page ends with a note that all three doors converge on the same editable cast and drafted opening.

## Quick start

Quick start is the shortest path and the product's first "wow" moment. The page asks for one line, the premise, with three example chips that fill the field. It exposes the three tone dials, comedic versus grim, grounded versus fantastical, and romance versus rivalry, each as a continuous slider with plain-language ends and a numeric readout. It asks whether the director will play from the wings or as a role, and if they choose a role, it asks for the character's name and optional pronouns. It exposes a cast-size stepper from one to eight with a "two to four recommended" hint and a live tier preview. A collapsed "More options" section contains genre, setting and era, presentation style (chat, script, or novel), and content to avoid. The primary button is "Cast and play." Submitting turns the page into a full-screen casting progress state with skeletons for the cast and a line of copy explaining that casting and the opening beat are being prepared. There is no progressive cast reveal yet; the audition appears after the cast and the opening draft are both ready.

## Guided setup

The guided wizard is four validated steps whose current step lives in the URL so it can be linked and reloaded. The progress indicator is a row of four pills: Premise, World and tone, You in scene, Cast size. The first step asks for the premise with examples. The second asks for genre, setting and era, presentation style, play language, the three tone dials, and content to avoid. The third asks whether the director is in the scene; choosing a role reveals name and pronouns and explains that the human card uses the same editor as the AI cast. The fourth asks for cast size with the same stepper and tier preview, and shows a compact summary of the number of AI roles, whether the director is also a role, the presentation style, and the premise. Back and Continue move through the steps; the final action is Generate cast. Templates enter this same wizard with a preset selected and the step set to cast, so the user can review or move backward through the seeded values.

## Templates

The template gallery presents six genre seeds: Noir interrogation, Court intrigue, Space crew, Campus drama, Cozy tavern, and Duel of letters. Each card shows the template name in serif, a metadata line with tone summary, cast size, and presentation style, a three-line premise, a row of story-role badges, and a collapsible cast skeleton with one-line hooks. The action is "Use this template," which opens the guided setup with the preset applied. The gallery is the most browse-oriented page in the product and is the natural place for richer genre imagery, sample art, or curated collections if the brand grows.

## Casting progress and the audition

The audition is the product's delight surface and its most information-dense screen. The same component serves quick start, guided setup, and templates, with a compact variant for quick start. The page header shows the scene title, premise, a badge with the number of active AI roles, a badge naming the human role if there is one, and a Draft badge. Above the cards is a full-width vibe steer bar: a free-text instruction such as "make the whole cast younger" or "more pressure, fewer speeches," a button to apply it to the whole cast, and a note that locked fields survive regeneration and changed cards get a badge. Errors appear in a dismissible alert above the grid, and non-blocking casting warnings appear in a muted list.

The cast itself is a responsive grid of role cards, two columns on compact screens and up to three on wide ones. Cards are reorderable by drag or keyboard, and the order is explained as spotlight priority. An active card shows a monogram or generated avatar with a deterministic gradient, the name, the occupation and pronouns, a story-role badge, a tier badge, and a changed badge when the last regeneration touched it. The card header also carries an identity lock, a drag handle, and a menu with Edit card, Hear a sample line, Duplicate role, Regenerate role, and Move off-stage. Inside the card are grouped field sections: Hook; Personality with traits, a secret, and temperament dials in the full editor; Voice with register and rhythm; Goals; and a collapsible Relationships list with dynamic and tension. Every field section has three tiny controls: regenerate, lock, and edit. The full editor hides the field controls for human roles. Off-stage roles collapse into a dashed card with a Restore action and a note that existing beats are untouched. The compact quick-start variant hides Voice and Goals to reduce noise.

Below the grid is a bar with the cast-size stepper, which recomputes the active AI count, and an "Add blank role" action. The stepper previews spotlight, supporting, and background tiers and explains the cost and pacing implications of larger casts inline, never in a modal. The audition's second major surface is the opening beat. It is a card at the bottom with a serif text area, a Draft badge, a reroll counter, and a word count. It offers Generate or Regenerate, "Write it myself," and Start scene. The opening is not canon until Start. Generate and reroll call the model, the text is editable, and Start commits the chosen text and navigates to the play console. There is also a loading skeleton state while the first opening is being drafted.

The role editor is a side sheet shared by AI and human roles. It organizes the full card into Identity, Hook, Personality, Voice, Goals and boundaries, Temperament, and Casting sections. Identity includes name, monogram, pronouns, age and era, occupation, and an accent color chosen from the eight identity slots. Personality includes traits, flaws, fears, desires, and a secret. Voice includes register, rhythm, verbosity, vocabulary notes, verbal tics, a do-not-say list, and narration style. Goals include public and secret goals, stakes, and lines the role will not cross. Temperament has four sliders. Casting exposes story role and tier. The sheet saves the whole card and explains that draft changes apply immediately while play revisions are versioned.

The sample-line sheet is a bottom sheet on every viewport. It shows the role's avatar and name, a line of copy explaining whether the lines are cached for this version of the card or freshly generated, two or three in-voice lines in serif quotation, and a "Write another" action. The sheet is focus-trapped and dismissible.

## Entering play

Start scene commits the opening, freezes the initial role revisions, and navigates to the play console. The first beat is visible in the scene's reading stream, and the floor indicator is already resolving the next speaker. From this moment the experience changes character: the audition was a review surface, and the play console is a live control room. The opening remains editable later, but editing it will fork the story rather than change the original.

## The play console

On a wide desktop the play console is a full-viewport composition under the global bar. A scene toolbar spans the top with a back arrow, the title, a status badge, the premise, a Cast button on smaller screens, Share, Export, and an Inspector toggle. Below it, the cast rail runs down the left at a fixed width, the story thread occupies the flexible center with a maximum reading measure, and the inspector, when open, occupies a fixed right column. On a tablet or phone the cast rail and inspector become side sheets, and the thread takes the full width. On a phone the scene toolbar and the floor indicator must share scarce vertical space, and the composer sits at the bottom with safe-area padding.

The cast rail is the social presence of the scene. It lists active roles with avatar, name, tier badge, story-role label, a speaking highlight, and a queued badge. Each AI role has a small menu with "Give floor now" and "Queue next." Off-stage roles are listed below in a muted section, with no action other than the implicit promise that they can be restored from the audition or settings. The rail turns the cast into a visible ensemble rather than a dropdown.

The floor indicator is the scene's heartbeat. It shows the current or next speaker's avatar, a status line such as "Rin is speaking," "Next: The Widow," "Three paths open, your direction," "Paused, auto-floor is off," or "Scene archived," and a secondary line with the burst counter, queued count, abort state, and pause note. On the right it can show an option count, Offer paths, Abort while a run is active, and Pause or Resume. It is deliberately compact and never renders per-token updates.

The thread is a virtualized reading column that can hold hundreds of beats. It sticks to the bottom while the director is at the bottom and shows a "Jump to latest" pill with a keyboard hint when they scroll up. Each beat is a card with a role-colored left border, an avatar, the speaker name, an origin badge such as Opening, Turn, Picked path, You wrote, Edited, or Regenerated, a pin badge, a "speaking" state, and a beat number. The body renders sanitized Markdown with typography that changes by presentation style: conversational sans for chat, monospaced screenplay formatting for script, and serif prose for novel. A streaming beat shows a caret and skeleton lines before any text arrives. A failed beat becomes a red-tinted card that explains the partial text was kept off the active path and offers Retry. The tip beat has a subtle ring, and a selected beat, reached from the inspector or map, has a stronger ring. The beat menu carries Pin, Edit (forks), Inspect prompts, and Regenerate (forks). A pinned beat is visually marked because pinned beats are always kept in context.

Path options appear inline under their parent beat, never in a modal. Each option card shows the speaker's avatar and name, a two-to-four-word label, a strategy chip such as escalate, de-escalate, reveal, conceal, ally, confront, commit, or deflect, and a two-line preview with a "Full text" disclosure. The active-tip options show a "Take this path" button and a keyboard hint for Alt or Option plus a number. Options offered at an older beat are collapsed behind a "N unused paths at this beat, Review" disclosure. Expanding them shows dashed cards with a fork warning: taking this path now forks the story from that beat and leaves the current branch intact, and the action becomes "Fork from here." This is the product's most important place to teach non-destructive branching, and the current implementation does it through copy and border treatment more than through spatial visualization.

A vertical branch gutter runs along the right edge of the thread on large screens. It maps the active path to dots, decision points to diamonds, and forks to colored chips at their divergence position. Clicking a dot scrolls to that beat; clicking a fork chip switches branches. It is hidden on smaller screens, so the only spatial overview there is the Map tab in the inspector. Branch switching shows a toast, "Context updated," with the explanation that memory now follows the other branch and nothing was regenerated.

Below the thread, the director bar carries Undo, Offer paths, Pause or Resume, and End scene. Undo is enabled only when the action log has an un-undone action, and toasts for reversible actions include their own Undo. Offer paths generates options at the current tip when the cadence has not produced them automatically. Pause stops the auto-floor but explicitly leaves direct actions available, which is a deliberate and slightly surprising rule that the copy must keep clear. End scene opens a confirmation dialog that explains wrapping runs a final summary pass, freezes the tree, opens the archive, deletes nothing, and can be undone. The scene can also surface a non-modal wrap offer banner after two consecutive resolution signals, with Wrap scene and Keep playing.

The composer is the director's writing desk. It is a segmented control between Steer and In-character. In Steer mode the placeholder invites an out-of-character note such as "make her suspicious" or "skip to the duel," with three quick chips for faster pace, raise the stakes, and cut to the chase, plus a Mention popover that searches active AI roles and forces one to speak, stealing the floor if a run is active. A "Steer and regenerate" action adds the note and forks a rewrite of the last beat. In-character mode shows a role selector defaulting to the human role or the first spotlight role, a text area, and a Write button that appends a user-insert beat. Active director notes appear above the composer as dismissible chips labeled steer, constraint, or do not. The send shortcut is Command or Control plus Enter. While a beat is streaming, in-character writing waits or requires an abort, while steering can be queued for the next beat. Archived scenes freeze the composer and explain that the tree is preserved.

## Branching, undo, and regeneration

Editing a beat opens a dialog with the beat text in a serif text area. The dialog explains that editing forks from the parent and preserves the original and everything after it, and the primary action is "Save and fork." Regenerating opens a confirmation that explains a new branch will be created from the beat's parent, the same role will take another pass, the original branch stays intact, and undo restores the previous tip. Taking a ghost option opens a similar fork confirmation. Every completed pick, edit, regeneration, pin, mute, or branch switch is logged and undoable through the action log, with a global Command or Control Z binding and toast-level Undo actions. The product never offers a destructive "delete." Off-stage, muted, retired, and artifact are soft states.

## The inspector

The inspector is a URL-driven pane with four tabs: Memory, Prompts, Map, and Usage. On a desktop it is the third column; on smaller screens it is a full-width right sheet. Its state lives in the query string, so a specific beat and tab can be linked. It can be toggled from the scene header or with a keyboard shortcut, and the last open tab is restored.

Memory shows the active path's working memory. Continuity warnings sit at the top in amber cards with severity and beat references and a Dismiss action. Facts in context follow, with global pins visually distinct because they cross branches by explicit override, then path facts, then collapsed disclosures for off-path facts and muted facts. Each fact row shows the text, the beat where it was introduced, subject names, pin and mute controls, and a global label when relevant. Summaries follow with their covered beat range. The panel is a model of the scene's knowledge, and its density is closer to a developer tool than to a story surface.

Prompts shows the exact compiled prompt for a selected beat or, when no beat is selected, the latest generations. A row of chips switches between snapshots by mode and relative time. The selected snapshot shows mode, model, token counts, context window, system target percentage, and any over-budget action. Below that, an accordion lists each block with its key, template key and version, tokens, characters, and a hash. Expanding a block shows the rendered prompt text in a monospaced preformatted block. This is the provenance surface, meant for power users and debugging, and it is the least "director-facing" part of the product.

The Map tab is a lazily loaded React Flow canvas laid out with dagre. Nodes are beats, colored by branch, labeled with the speaker name, sequence number, origin, status, path count, and pinned state. The active path is emphasized and inactive branches are dimmed. Edges distinguish canon from alternatives. Selecting a node scrolls the thread and can inspect the beat; switching a branch is possible from the map. A keyboard-navigable node list mirrors the graph for accessibility. Behind a flag, a read-only compare mode aligns two branches side by side with divergence markers.

Usage shows estimated cost, tokens in and out, and a per-tier table. If the scene has a soft dollar cap, a progress bar and a warning at eighty percent and one hundred percent explain that generation is never hard-blocked. The copy repeatedly labels the numbers as estimates.

## Ending, archive, and export

Wrapping a scene runs a final summary pass over uncovered beats, archives the scene, and opens the archive route. The play console also lets a director open the archive while the scene is still playing, where it acts as a live read-only snapshot. The archive page has a header with the title, status, premise, genre and era, presentation style, ended time or a note that this is a live snapshot, estimated cost, and token total. It offers Share and Export. A branch explorer row shows every branch as a colored chip with its beat count; selecting one replays that branch. The replay is a read-only chain from root to tip with role accents, origin badges, pinned markers, edited-fork notes, and a collapsed "N paths not taken" disclosure for ghosts. The ledger below shows facts, summaries, director notes, and continuity warnings in two columns. There are no mutate affordances anywhere on this page.

The export dialog offers two formats. Markdown is a screenplay-ish read with the mainline first and branch deltas after it. JSON is a lossless tree with ids, branch links, candidates, facts, and optional prompt snapshots. Files are generated in the browser and download immediately. The include-snapshots switch is explained as adding the compiled prompts. The share dialog sits beside export and is available from both the play console and the archive.

## Sharing and presence

A share link is the entire credential. The share dialog offers two permission cards, View only, which streams the scene live read-only, and Can direct, which can also pick path options and nothing else. Creating a link copies it immediately and keeps the URL visible in a list. Active links show permission, URL, creation time, Copy, and Revoke. Revoked and expired links move to an Inactive section. The copy is explicit that the scene itself is untouched by revocation and that links are the only credential a visitor needs.

The shared page shows the title, a badge that says Can pick paths or View only, an archived badge when relevant, the premise, and a presence badge with viewer initials and "N watching." A slim floor strip shows who is speaking or what the scene is waiting for. The thread is the same reading surface as the play console but strips every mutate affordance for viewers; director links add only path picking. A footer restates the permission and the promise that nothing is deleted. Revocation closes the door immediately, and the revoked visitor sees a calm empty state with a path back to their own scene. Presence names are stable per tab so reloads do not inflate the count.

## Cross-cutting states, keyboard, and interaction craft

Every surface is expected to have loading, empty, error, offline, and reconnecting states. The shared primitives are skeletons, centered empty states with an icon, title, description and action, an error state with a retry and a reassurance that the story is safe, and slim offline and reconnecting banners. Route-level error boundaries give surface-specific copy and never imply data loss. The play console disables mutating controls while offline and queues actions through Convex on reconnect. The scene shell shows loading skeletons while the scene opens. Failed generations leave no beat before the first token, and a mid-stream failure leaves a retryable row. Option generation can drop individual failures and retries the batch if fewer than two succeed. The inspector and map have their own skeletons and panel-level boundaries.

The keyboard layer is a registry, not an afterthought. Command or Control K opens the command palette, which currently contains navigation, appearance, and help commands. Command or Control Z undoes the last director action. Command or Control J jumps to the latest beat. Alt or Option plus one through four picks the corresponding path option. Command or Control Shift O offers paths. Command or Control period aborts generation. Command or Control Enter sends from the composer. Command or Control I toggles the inspector, B opens the map, and M opens memory. Question mark opens the shortcuts dialog, and Escape closes the topmost context. The shortcuts dialog renders directly from the registry, and collisions are tested in CI.

The motion system is quiet and purposeful: skeleton shimmer, streaming caret, pulsing optimistic beats, sheet and dialog transitions, and a reduced-motion variant for all of them. Toasts sit bottom-right and carry undo. Focus is trapped in modal surfaces, and the skip link is the first element in the DOM. The reading column is capped at a seventy-two-character measure, and the typography is deliberately small and dense in control areas, with larger serif type reserved for prose and key headings.

## Visual language today

The default theme is dark: a near-black stage background, slightly lifted card surfaces, warm parchment text, a muted warm gray for secondary text, and a single amber primary that carries the brand. The light theme is warm parchment with dark ink and a darker amber. The eight role accents are amber, teal, violet, rose, sky, lime, orange, and indigo; each has a paired foreground and is used consistently for avatars, beat borders, branch colors, and map nodes. Surfaces are mostly one-pixel borders with soft shadows, medium radii around ten pixels, and cards rather than heavy chrome. Display and prose use a serif; UI and controls use a system sans; script presentation uses monospace. Small uppercase tracked labels act as section markers, and badges are used heavily for status, origin, tier, strategy, and metadata.

The current aesthetic is best described as a literary salon crossed with a technical console. The story surfaces are warm, editorial, and readable. The control surfaces are dense, small, and instrument-like. The tension between those two languages is the biggest visual opportunity. The play console currently stacks a global bar, a scene toolbar, a wrap banner, a floor indicator, a thread, a director bar, and a composer, which leaves the story a relatively small vertical window on laptops and phones. The inspector is powerful but reads like a debugging panel, with hashes, token counts, and ten-pixel metadata. The audition is rich but crowded, with three tiny icon buttons per field section and a large number of equal-weight actions. A redesign can preserve the stage-and-parchment identity while giving the story more room and making the power surfaces progressive rather than permanent.

## Pages that are missing and should be designed

The shipped product has no scene library. A returning director can only reach a scene through a bookmark or browser history, even though the backend can list a user's scenes. The most important new page is therefore a home or library for the signed-in or returning director: recent scenes, grouped by playing, paused, and archived, with a card that shows the title, premise, cast, last beat excerpt, status, branch count, and last activity, plus resume, archive, share, and export actions. It needs a designed empty state that leads to the three doors, a loading skeleton, an error retry, and a search or filter once the list grows. This page is also the natural home for the product's identity after the first session, and it should probably replace the value screen for returning visitors without hiding the promise from new ones.

There is no scene overview or settings page after creation. A director cannot rename a scene, revisit the premise and tone, change presentation style, adjust boundaries or cadence, manage the cast outside the audition, see a recap, or find the share and export actions in one place. The scene toolbar is the only persistent chrome, and it is optimized for live play. A scene overview should collect the title and premise, a cast strip with roles and tiers, the story settings, branch and memory summaries, share links, exports, and a clear path into play, the map, and the archive. It should also make the scene lifecycle legible: draft, playing, paused, wrapped, archived. The current language of End scene, Wrap scene, Archive, and Paused is functionally precise but conceptually muddy, and a lifecycle diagram or a single "scene status" surface would help.

There is no account surface. Anonymous sessions are the only supported identity in the UI, the guest banner explains that scenes live in the browser, and the copy still says account saving arrives with sharing even though sharing has shipped. The backend already has an anonymous-to-account claim mutation. A redesign needs a light account and claim flow: a sign-in or create-account screen that can adopt the current guest scenes, a profile surface with display name and theme preferences, a clear explanation of where scenes live, and a sign-out that warns about anonymous data. It should remain strictly optional and never interrupt the first scene.

There is no help or about surface beyond the shortcuts dialog and inline copy. The product's best ideas, non-destructive branching, ghosts, memory scoping, the three directing modes, and the floor, are explained in fragments. A help surface should teach the mental model with the same care as the product: a short "how to direct" tour that can be replayed, a branch-law explainer with a small interactive fork example, a glossary that uses only product language, and answers to questions about privacy, cost, sharing, and self-hosting. The command palette is the natural place to expose it.

The planned co-play release adds pages and surfaces that should be designed now so the shell can grow without a rewrite. It needs a seats and invite surface where the host can bind humans to roles, choose player or spectator permission, and see who is present; a human turn state that says "Your turn" with pass and optional timer controls; a spectator shell that extends the shared page with presence and reactions off by default; and a private-knowledge editor where facts and whispers can be scoped to roles, with collapsed rendering for anyone not entitled to them. It also needs a branch compare and merge surface, branch renaming and notes, and text search across branches. The inspector is the natural home for compare and notes, while seats and invites belong with the cast rail and the scene overview.

The planned community release adds a gallery, publishing, remixing, moderation, audio, localization, and a spend center. The gallery needs a discovery page with genre and tone filters, scene cards with premise, cast preview, and a first-beat excerpt, and a public read page with social metadata. Publishing needs a dialog that explains what becomes public and what never does, since facts and play history never travel with a remix. Remixing needs an attribution banner and a copy of premise, settings, and cast skeleton only. Moderation needs report controls, a review queue, and a takedown state. Audio adds a per-beat play control, a radio-mode transport, captions, and a voice picker on the role card, and it must never block the text path. The spend center adds per-scene and per-account budgets, trends, and alerts. Localization adds a language setting and an RTL audit, while the scene's play language remains a per-scene setting. The archived reading experience should eventually become installable and readable offline, with no offline editing.

## What a redesign must preserve

A redesign can change the layout, the visual identity, the component shapes, and the navigation, but it must preserve the branch laws and make them perceptible. It must keep path options inline with their parent beat, keep editing and regeneration as forks, keep ghosts recoverable, keep undo honest, and keep the distinction between canonical beats, failed attempts, artifacts, and pending picks. It must keep the three directing modes distinct and available. It must keep the all-AI cast explicit and never let a meta system speak in fiction. It must keep guest-first entry, the absence of an account wall, the self-hostable deployment model, the product vocabulary, and the accessibility bar. It must keep the story readable at a seventy-two-character measure with a type system that can express chat, script, and novel prose. And it must keep the performance contract: a virtualized thread that stays smooth at two hundred beats, a lazy map, and streaming that survives a reload without duplicate text.

The largest design opportunity is not a new skin. It is to make the director's console feel like one coherent place. Today the product is a strong set of surfaces with a missing front door, a missing account story, a dense audition, and a powerful but technical inspector. A better design would give the director a home, a clear scene overview, a calmer play surface with more room for prose, a more legible branch model, a more progressive disclosure of power features, and a mobile experience that does not merely collapse the desktop console but offers its own spatial and narrative overview. The north star is a director who can feel the room, see the shape of the story, and trust that every path they did not take is still there.
